Finishing the Melody — reading Slow Technology (Hallnäs & Redström)

The things that stick in my mind from this article aren't the whole philosophy of slow technology and designing for reflection, the part that forces the user to feel the presence of time. It's the smaller things. The doorbell example, for one.

Reading Slow Technology - Designing For Reflection by Hallnäs & Redström Link

We used to have exactly this slow doorbell when I was a kid. It was always weird hearing it play fragments of a melody each time. I can't tell if it was annoying — but I remember completing the melody every time it stopped.

Another one is this paragraph:

Why is it not enough to have a reminder sign on the wall saying in capital letters ''SMILE'' or ''THINK OF YOUR FAVOURITE PAINTING BY MATISSE'', etc? A key reason why this substitution is pointless is that the reminder sign is very imprecise in telling me what my favourite painting by Matisse is or why I should smile.

One thing I think the authors themselves seemed worried about was the distinction between art and the moment when you say: "Ok, so what?" It kept crossing my mind too — even though each time, I had my own answers. The one I keep coming back to: slow doesn't mean it stops being a tool. If the slowness comes from concentrating on aesthetics, the same object can still do its job — it just does it while making you reflect. The doorbell still tells me someone's at the door. It only changes how I receive that.

Slow technology has to be carefully framed and introduced in order not to be perceived merely as some poorly designed and, as a result, useless tool.

Some parts of fast technology can still be slow and annoying. As mentioned in the article, the learning curve — understanding why it works, how it works, applying it, seeing what it is, and finding out the consequences — can all be frustrating, and a result of bad design.

But this slowness is appealing in time-based art forms, like music, which are designed for reflection.

Maybe that's the distinction I keep landing on: annoying slowness is slowness you didn't choose. The doorbell made me wait — but it also handed me a melody to finish. One asks for nothing back. The other leaves room for you.

That room it leaves you to reflect is, I guess, what slow technology brings.
