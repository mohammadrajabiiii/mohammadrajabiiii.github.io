Protected, Controlled Bravery

Notes from Piranesi

A few days ago, I finished reading Susanna Clarke’s Piranesi. This isn't about my ideas on the book, a book review, or anything like that.

For you to have some background, Piranesi lives in a very big house full of giant statues and weird things going on, by himself and another person called the Other, with whom he has very little contact. There had lived 13 people before, Piranesi being 14, and the Other being 15. One day the Other tells Piranesi that someone new will be coming here. Looking for you. But you have to hide from them, for their words can make you mad. I will not get into the why and how of the story.

There is a passage that I keep thinking about:

“It is not the fact that 16 is a woman that fascinates and excites me — or at least not entirely; it is the fact that she is another human being. I want to learn everything I can about her — or as much as I can learn without going mad. (That is the tricky part.) (p. 97-98)”

You see, the desire to discover another human being is tempting and beautiful. However, there is another part, another layer, that stayed with me: The fragility.

When he says, “… or as much as I can learn without going mad”, it fascinates me. He knows that learning more could drive him mad, and he is afraid of that. And he still wants to experience it. And at the same time, he wants to protect himself. He wants to go as far as he can without going mad.

I think there is something familiar about that feeling.

Whenever we approach something unfamiliar, such as a person, a relationship, or an experience, there is a certain amount of risk involved. We want to know what is on the other side, yet we also know whatever we find might change us. It might hurt us. Disappoint us. Reject us. It might be anything we are not prepared to discover. And yet, we move closer.

I like this protected, controlled bravery that Piranesi has: Knowing that something might hurt you, understanding your own limits, but choosing to approach anyway.

I like the way he knows his limits, is afraid of them, and still chooses to approach his desires carefully.

It isn’t about throwing yourself into the unknown without thinking. Nor is it keeping everything at a safe distance. You stand somewhere in between.

Perhaps this is one of the most intriguing things about being human. We want to get close, but closeness makes us fragile and vulnerable.

ChatGPT told me that a Danish philosopher, Søren Kierkegaard, has written about anxiety and possibility. One of his interesting ideas is that to have possibilities means to have something to lose.

I like when human beings become fragile. I like when they make confessions of their weakness. I like when Piranesi simply admits that he might go mad.

Sometimes those are more moving than declarations of strength.

There is a song by Vic Chesnutt that I keep remembering: “The courage of the coward is greater than all others […], and I am a coward.”

I wonder if there is a difference between (a) knowing your limits and (b) believing you know your limits.
