Likable, Not Lovable

11 November 2025

A life lesson I learned at the navy

Chapter I: Setup — The Uniform of Anonymity

It was the last days of fall 2024. I had a final party with my colleagues, said goodbye to them, and flew to my hometown to say goodbye to my family and friends. The final destination was the north, for Military Service. The deadline I ran and scared my whole late teenage-hood had finally come.

I felt numb. It didn't take much for me to find myself inside the Navy uniform, floating in the shared pool of identity. You enter the army as nobody. Everyone looks alike. Shaved head, identical uniforms, the smell of wax, and the mourning of the boot leathers. Everybody even sounds the same.

I felt average. There was nothing I could judge people by, which used to help me decide how friendly or guarded I wanted to be. The blend-in made me feel average. But at one point, I started thinking about how bold my differences could be, because I was disarmed. No stylish clothes or facial hair to distract people's attention from how short or fat I might be. And the struggle hit me, along with all the other things I had to confront.

Chapter II: The Ascent — Earning the Spotlight

The numbness didn't last. It took a couple of days to shake off the shock. Then, the realization hit: "Me" is not (only) my appearance. There was something deeper inside that was truly "me," something the uniform couldn't mask.

My defense mechanism, honed during school days, began to activate: humor and sarcasm. I knew how to joke, how to cope with a group I was compelled to live with. It was a successful strategy: to be friends with everyone, and yet, ultimately, with no one. This allowed me to put up borders, ensuring I never got too friendly with anyone. This control over my distance feels cool and strategic (Haha!), but the truth is, I never can get too friendly with anyone. Something always stops me.

Soon, I was this famous person in the barracks. The tipping point arrived when the unit held a vote for the Mental Health Assistant. I volunteered. And almost everybody voted for me. That was a nice moment, seeing all those hands raised for me. I immediately confessed I had just joked about the idea, expecting anger, but they just liked me more — they enjoyed the chaos, the unexpected.

My reputation grew. I sang for them, made them laugh non-stop, and bantered with the few I was closer to. One day, the captain casually mentioned that I teach at the university which even added more to the fame. The weird, unexpected personality they had elected. They liked the company, they liked the contradiction. They liked me, the character.

Chapter III: Climax — The Distance of Deep Connection

The attention was constant, and it exhausted me. All the time, they came to me to talk — about their loved ones, how they missed their families, their pain, or who they really were outside. I took notes, listened, and wrote, learning how to cope with the people I didn't like, or even hated, out of pure necessity. I was the safe receptacle for their secrets, but I gave none in return.

I remembered the parting words of my therapist: that I never let people get very close to me. Something about intimacy frightens me. Being liked, I realized, was an easy trick to perform. I was charismatic, funny, and helpful. Likability was transactional; it relied on my performance.

The distinction hit me hardest during one late night. Something bad had happened, and when I tried to talk with a friend about the real anxiety about my future, I burst into tears. He smiled, clapped me on the shoulder, and said: "Don't worry, man, you're funny and smart. You'll figure it out."

When I looked out at the faces who liked me, I saw admiration for the role, but never the deep, unguarded intimacy I truly craved. I felt the sharp, undeniable truth:

I am a likable person, but this likability never leads into a deeper connection. I am likable, but not lovable.

The loud success of my military fame suddenly felt deafeningly silent. All the past relationships — the ones where I was told I "didn't try enough" — flashed across my mind. I had never gone the crucial step further. I was so good at being liked, I never learned how to be vulnerable enough to be loved.

Chapter IV: Conclusion — The Weight of the Insight

The barracks became the place where I had a profound realization. Likability is a currency you earn through performance; lovability is a surrender you achieve through vulnerability. I had perfected the earning, but was terrified of the surrender.

What I learned under the rigid military structure is the most important lesson I took from that time. It defined a new search: a search to understand the difference between the friend you want to laugh with and the one you are willing to cry with. The tension between the person everyone liked, and the person who was still waiting to be loved.
